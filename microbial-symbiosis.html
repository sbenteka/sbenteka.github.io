Research Area

Microbial Symbiosis

My interest in microbial symbiosis grew from a long-standing curiosity about Actinobacteria and the different strategies bacteria use to persist, compete, and function in complex environments. Frankia-actinorhizal associations provided a particularly compelling system: an actinobacterium that not only survives in soil, but forms highly specialized, nitrogen-fixing partnerships with plants.

Frankia & Actinorhizal Plants

From bacterial morphology to host-selected symbiosis

This research formed a major component of my doctoral work, where I investigated how Frankia populations are distributed in soil, how they can be quantified accurately, and how those environmental populations relate to the strains ultimately found inside nitrogen-fixing root nodules.

Frankia are filamentous actinobacteria that occur in soil and can establish nitrogen-fixing root nodules with a diverse group of woody actinorhizal plants. Their biology sits at an interesting intersection of microbial ecology, plant-microbe interactions, and nitrogen cycling.

This system allowed me to approach symbiosis from an ecological perspective: which Frankia populations are present in soil, which become enriched around plant roots, and which are ultimately selected into nodules? Answering those questions required both quantitative molecular tools and direct comparison of environmental and symbiotic populations.

Why this system interested me

My broader research has repeatedly returned to Actinobacteria because of their remarkable ecological diversity. In extremophile systems, the question is often how bacteria tolerate environmental stress. In symbiosis, the complementary question is how bacteria succeed through highly specific biological interactions with a host.

Quantifying Frankia in Soil

Building tools to measure environmental populations

Before asking which Frankia populations plants select, we first needed reliable ways to determine which populations were present in soil and in what abundance.

Applied and Environmental Microbiology · 2017

Sybr Green- and TaqMan-based qPCR approaches for Frankia clusters

Ben Tekaya S, Ganesan AS, Guerra T, Dawson JO, Forstner MRJ, Hahn D.

This study expanded molecular quantification of Frankia by developing cluster-specific qPCR approaches for clusters 2 and 4, complementing existing assays for clusters 1 and 3. The resulting framework allowed the major taxonomic groups within the genus to be assessed together rather than focusing only on the historically better-characterized clusters.

Applying these assays to different soils showed that vegetation was associated with striking differences in detectable Frankia community composition. Prairie soils contained representatives of all four major clusters and showed broader diversity, while soils beneath some host and nonhost trees were dominated by a much more restricted subset of populations.

Key idea: understanding symbiosis starts before nodulation. The environmental pool of potential symbionts has to be measured first.

From Soil to Root Nodules

Does abundance in soil predict symbiotic success?

The next question was more ecological: do the Frankia populations that dominate the rhizosphere also dominate inside root nodules?

Applied and Environmental Microbiology · 2018

Frankia diversity in root nodules versus corresponding rhizosphere soils

Ben Tekaya S, Guerra T, Rodriguez D, Dawson JO, Hahn D.

Using plant microcosms, qPCR, targeted Illumina sequencing of soil populations, and sequence analysis of nodule-associated Frankia, we compared the diversity present in rhizosphere soils with the populations actually recovered from root nodules.

The result was a clear ecological disconnect. The populations most abundant in soil were not necessarily the populations recovered from nodules, and several nodule-associated sequences were rare or undetected in the corresponding soils. This indicated that root nodule formation could not be explained simply by the abundance or relative diversity of Frankia populations in the surrounding rhizosphere.

Key idea: symbiosis acts as a biological filter. Host association can favor particular microbial partners rather than simply reflecting the most abundant organisms in the environment.

Ecology through interaction

This work shaped how I think about microbial ecology more broadly. Environmental persistence is not governed only by intrinsic stress tolerance or metabolic capacity. It is also shaped by biological interactions, host selection, competition, and access to specialized ecological niches. The Frankia-actinorhizal system gave me a framework for studying those interactions quantitatively, from environmental populations in soil to the highly selective environment of the root nodule.
