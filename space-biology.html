Research Area

Space Biology

Space biology asks an unusually fundamental question: how far can life be pushed beyond the environmental conditions under which it evolved? My work approaches this question through stress-tolerant biological systems, currently using the tardigrade Hypsibius exemplaris as a model for investigating responses to spaceflight and associated environmental stress. More broadly, I am interested in connecting animal stress biology with extremotolerant microorganisms, microbial interactions, and questions of habitability, with the longer-term goal of understanding how biological systems might persist, function, and support one another beyond Earth.

Current Research

Tardigrades as a system for studying biological resilience

Tardigrades provide a compelling model for asking how complex organisms respond to unusual environmental conditions, including spaceflight and other forms of physiological stress. My current work in this area integrates comparative stress biology with transcriptomic analysis and functional interpretation.

Why Tardigrades?

Exceptional resilience, from whole-organism survival to molecular protection

Tardigrades have become iconic models of biological resilience because some species can tolerate severe dehydration, freezing, high doses of ionizing radiation, vacuum, and other environmental extremes. Their relevance to space biology is not merely theoretical: experiments during the FOTON-M3 mission showed that desiccated tardigrades could survive direct exposure to space vacuum, with some animals also recovering after combined exposure to vacuum and solar radiation.[1] Follow-up work by Rebecchi and colleagues further examined the responses of both active and desiccated tardigrades flown in low Earth orbit.[2]

Their extraordinary stress tolerance is also associated with unusual molecular adaptations. Tardigrade-specific intrinsically disordered proteins, including CAHS proteins, contribute to survival during severe water loss and can enhance desiccation tolerance in heterologous systems.[4] Other tardigrade proteins reveal complementary strategies for coping with DNA damage: Dsup can reduce radiation-associated DNA damage in cultured human cells[3], while the more recently characterized TDR1 is strongly induced following ionizing radiation and has been implicated in preserving DNA organization while repair proceeds.[5]

Together, this combination of whole-organism resilience and distinctive molecular protection makes tardigrades unusually powerful models for asking how complex biological systems respond to conditions relevant to spaceflight.

Space biology is a natural extension of my broader interest in organisms that function under severe environmental challenge. In H. exemplaris, the same questions that motivate my work on terrestrial stress tolerance can be asked in the context of spaceflight: how are stress-response programs reorganized, which protective mechanisms are deployed, and how do those responses change across environmental contexts?

My role in this research has focused strongly on the computational and comparative side, including analysis of complex transcriptomic datasets and integration of stress-response patterns across experimental conditions.

Why this fits my broader research

The same question runs through much of my work: how does life persist when conditions become difficult? In one system that may mean desiccation or radiation tolerance, in another it may mean host association or nutrient acquisition, and in space biology it becomes a question of resilience under environments far removed from ordinary terrestrial conditions.

Future Directions

From stress tolerance to habitability

My longer-term goal is to connect stress biology with broader questions of how biological systems might function in environments relevant to future space exploration.

Extremotolerant microorganisms

I am interested in using stress-tolerant bacterial isolates as experimental systems for understanding responses to space-relevant challenges such as radiation, altered gravity, desiccation, and resource limitation.

Microbial support of plant systems

A particularly exciting direction is asking whether beneficial or stress-tolerant microorganisms can help plants establish and function under difficult growth conditions, including lunar and Martian regolith simulants.

Habitability as an interaction problem

Rather than viewing habitability only as the tolerance of an individual organism, I am interested in how microbial communities and plant-microbe partnerships may help create more resilient biological systems in extraterrestrial environments.

Where my research axes converge

My longer-term interest is in understanding how stress-tolerant organisms and beneficial microbial interactions can contribute to biological resilience beyond Earth. This creates a natural bridge between my work in extremophile biology, microbial symbiosis, and space biology. Across these systems, the central question is the same: how can life persist, function, and interact when environmental conditions move far beyond the range normally considered favorable?
